Local gym redesigns changing spaces after hearing from transgender members

By Courier Staff 6 min read

By the time Mike Reynolds gets to Riverview Fitness after work, he usually has one thing on his mind: getting through his workout before dinner.

The 46-year-old mechanic has belonged to gyms for most of his adult life. He likes familiar routines — free weights, a short stretch, home. So when Riverview announced that it was renovating its locker rooms and moving to a shared changing area with private rooms and showers, he paid attention.

“I’ll be straight with you, I wasn’t sure about it at first,” Reynolds said. “I grew up with a men’s locker room and a women’s locker room. That’s what I knew. When I heard transgender members would be using the same shared changing area, I had questions.”

His concerns were practical, he said.

“Are people going to be changing in front of each other? What happens if somebody feels uncomfortable? What are the rules? I didn’t want asking those questions to automatically mean somebody thought I had something against transgender people. I wanted to know how it was actually going to work.”

Riverview, an independent community gym, reopened the changing area this spring after several months of renovations and conversations with members.

The new space has a common locker area, but changing rooms and showers are fully enclosed and lock from the inside. Members are expected to be clothed or covered in the common area. The same rules about privacy, personal space and respectful conduct apply to everyone using the facility.

Staff have also received additional training on responding to harassment, conflicts and questions about the gym’s policies.

Near the changing area, a small sign states that transgender and gender-diverse people are welcome to use the facility and its changing spaces.

Manager Elena Park said the changes came partly from conversations with transgender members who described thinking about things other members may rarely have to consider.

“Some people told us they were worried about privacy and about what a shared changing area would actually look like,” Park said. “Transgender members were telling us something different — that they worried about being stared at, challenged or made to feel that they were in the wrong place. We didn’t think the answer was to pretend those concerns were the same, or that either one wasn’t real.”

For Reynolds, seeing the space made a difference.

“I think I pictured something different in my head,” he said after a recent workout. “Nobody is changing out in the open. I go into a private room, somebody else goes into another one, and then we come out and get on with our workouts. Once I understood that, most of what I was worried about wasn’t really an issue.”

Jordan Lee, 31, has also been using gyms for years.

“Being trans means I sometimes walk into a changing room already thinking about whether somebody is going to question why I’m there,” she said. “You notice who’s looking at you. You wonder if someone thinks you’re in the wrong place. Sometimes you’re doing all of that before you’ve even started your workout.”

Lee said she does not expect every member of a gym to understand her experience.

“I don’t need everyone here to know what being trans is like,” she said. “I just want to know that I’m allowed to be here without it becoming a whole thing. I want to change, put my headphones on and work out.”

Park said that was one reason Riverview decided to make its policy explicit.

“If we only say ‘everyone is welcome,’ some people still don’t know whether that actually includes them,” she said. “For transgender members, being named can make that clearer. At the same time, we can acknowledge that other members may have questions about privacy or about something that feels unfamiliar. Those conversations don’t have to start from the assumption that somebody is acting in bad faith.”

Otherwise, little about Riverview feels unusual. The gym has rows of cardio machines, resistance equipment, free weights and a studio used for group classes. On weekday evenings, members move between sets, check their phones and wait for equipment to open up.

Reynolds said the renovation has become less noticeable with time.

“Most people are just here doing their workout,” he said. “That’s pretty much what I’m here for too.”
